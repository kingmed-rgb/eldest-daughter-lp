## **the weight you never chose** 

after sophia scooped a prestigious scholarship to yale, she was certain her life would never be the same. the connections she would build! the lifetime friends she would make! the career opportunities she would find! and the chance yale offered her to study abroad, broaden her perspective on life and make her into a more well-rounded person! it was going to be epic. 

as the summer slowly turned into fall, sophia marked off each day on the calendar, excited for her move to connecticut. but, slowly, as the clock wound down, sophia’s excitement morphed into trepidation. from trepidation, she moved to fear and, just a week before her move, sophia was carrying a 50lb bag of dread on her back, unable to breathe. after she awoke on an ambulance stretcher, a kind weathered face informed her that she had suffered a serious panic attack, sophia was no longer able to pretend that everything was fine. 

acknowledging a problem existed, however, was not enough to make it go away, and sophia left the hospital that night with her bag of dread significantly heavier. on the ride home, sophia’s mom was fuming. 

_“after all your father and i have done for you, this is how you repay us? do you know how much your father and i sacrificed to get you this far? there is no room for panic attacks at yale! you are enrolled at one of the most prestigious universities in the world, sophia! don’t blow this like you did your fifth grade piano recital. when i think about how much i spent on piano lessons only for you_ 

4 

_to freeze up on stage. only for you to give up on it completely. that’s the problem with you, sophia. i’ve been telling you since you were a child, but it’s like it goes in one ear and comes out the other. you have got to learn to commit to things if you want to be successful in life. you have to learn to give it 200% or don’t bother doing it. you can’t just quit halfway or end up in the hospital every time things get hard. life is hard. suck it up or you’ll never amount to anything.”_ 

for an eldest daughter like sophia, these words cut deeper than most. she had spent her childhood being **“the strong one,”** the responsible one, the example for her siblings. now, even in her moment of crisis, her mother saw only duty, performance, and sacrifice. 

the night before her move, sophia cried herself to sleep. what should have been one of the greatest memories of her burgeoning adulthood became a nightmare she couldn’t escape from. on campus, it seemed that every time she tried to make friends or answer a question in seminars, there was an invisible force stopping her. loudly, inside of her, she heard threats of her worthlessness being screamed at her all the time. 

during her second-year exams, sophia began to suffer from severe panic attacks again. it was as though the more she studied, the more she heard her mother’s voice telling her she wasn’t good enough. it wasn’t long before the panic returned. 

during a very important exam, sophia entered the exam hall trembling so badly that her professor asked if she was okay. although she nodded, all she could see was the faces of concern and disappointment in the audience as she froze in front of her piano. though seated in the exam hall, sophia was far away in the past, unable to play the piano for her fifth-grade recital. 

5 

_“do you know how much your father and i sacrificed to get you this far? there is no room for panic attacks at yale! you are enrolled at one of the most prestigious universities in the world, sophia!”_ 

her mother invaded her thoughts once more. despite sophia trying her hardest to silence that voice, it was too late. her heart was beating so loudly, she could no longer hear a thing. as her world began spinning into blackness, sophia saw her mother frowning at her sternly. 

thanks to the encouragement and kind words of her professor, sophia was able to complete the exam after all. still, she realized that she could no longer continue like this. what if this happened while taking more important exams in years to come? sophia had to finally face the truth. this invisible force was not going to go away until she faced the issue head-on. 

after years of trying to will her problems away, the aspiring lawyer finally booked an appointment with a therapist. she was finally ready to face the simple, but terrifying question that had been plaguing her her entire life: 

_“what’s wrong with me?”_ 

sophia had not yet put two and two together, but her therapist was about to explain. it was time for sophia to meet the part of herself shaped not just by childhood, but by the invisible weight of being the eldest daughter. 

the eldest daughter wound isn’t a diagnosis you’ll find in a medical textbook, but it is a lived reality, etched into your nervous system. it shows up in how you move through the world: always responsible, always reliable, always on alert. 

the eldest daughter wound is what happens when your worth is measured in usefulness, when love feels tied to performance, and when rest feels like laziness instead of healing. it’s years of being the strong one, the dependable one, the one who carries everyone else before herself. 

6 

this wound leaves traces everywhere: in the way you apologize for existing, in the way you feel guilty for wanting more, in the way you struggle to ask for help because deep down you’ve learned that no one is coming. it is not just stress or personality. it is an identity shaped by duty, guilt, and invisible contracts you never agreed to sign. 

that’s the eldest daughter wound: a story written on your body and your mind, convincing you that being everything for everyone else is the only way to be enough. 

but this wound didn’t come from nowhere. it has roots, in family roles, in unspoken expectations, in patterns passed down long before you. to understand how to heal, we have to understand how it was created.

## **where it all started** 

growing up with a highly critical mother had not been easy for sophia. whenever she slipped up, her mother, mel, would berate her and pile on guilt. mel would shame her and tell her she wasn’t trying hard enough. 

failure was not an option in sophia’s home, not if she wanted her mom’s love and approval. and emotions? those were treated as weakness. when sophia cried after losing a volleyball game or felt crushed for not winning that music competition, mel would scold her. if sophia had just “done better,” as mel insisted, she wouldn’t feel so bad. according to her mother, the only way to earn peace and pride was to always win, never fail, never make mistakes. 

by adulthood, sophia looked like she had it all together: driven, dependable, always the one others leaned on. but inside, a part of her still carried the weight of being “the responsible one” since childhood. this was the eldest daughter wound. it was like those cartoon storylines where a tiny character sits at the controls of a much bigger body. for sophia, that little controller was the part of her that had grown up too fast, the **“eldest daughter self”** who had learned to survive by being perfect, useful, and responsible. 

in psychology, this mirrors what many call the inner child, the part of you that holds on to childhood memories, fears, and unmet needs. for eldest daughters, that inner child often looks more like a little parent: the girl who had to grow up before she was ready, who learned that her worth came from caregiving, overachieving, and keeping the family together. 

10 

as children, we’re vulnerable. we rely on parents not just to feed us, but to show us that our emotions are valid and that we are safe. imagine a child falling in the park. if the mother comforts her, the child learns, “i’m safe. i’m loved. the world can be good.” but if the mother yells, dismisses, or shames her, the child learns, “i’m unworthy. my feelings don’t matter. the world isn’t safe.” 

for eldest daughters, this cycle is intensified. instead of being comforted, they’re often told to **“set a good example,”** to be strong, or to stop making things harder for their parents. the message seeps in: love is conditional. worth must be earned. and once those beliefs take root in a young, malleable brain, they don’t let go easily. 

this is why so many eldest daughters carry invisible weight into adulthood: still hustling for worth, still believing mistakes make them unlovable, still taking care of everyone else while ignoring their own needs. 

so, how did we get here? 

## **reason 1: parentification** 

it begins with the invisible long list of unpaid jobs you never chose. being the eldest daughter means you are the default adult. the one who wakes siblings up for school, ties their shoes, remembers the birthdays, calms the fights, packs the lunches, and sometimes even consoles a parent when the weight of life crushes them. you become the “third parent” before you even know what childhood is supposed to feel like. sometimes they call you “too mature for your age,” and you thought that was something to be praised for. 

psychologists call this **parentification** , when a child steps into adult roles because no one else can. eldest daughters are its most common victims. and 

11 

while it can make you reliable, empathetic, and strong, it also leaves scars: exhaustion, hyper-independence, and the aching sense that rest is laziness. 

many eldest daughters never truly felt like children. some remember cooking meals at nine years old; others recall standing between their parents during fights, shouting _“stop fighting!”_ childhood became something to manage, not something to live. 

it’s not just the chores. it’s also the emotional labor: making sure your siblings are okay, checking if your parents are in a good mood, anticipating conflict before it erupts and adjusting yourself to prevent it. you learn to read rooms like a weather report, hyper-vigilant, constantly scanning for storms. 

because you were always responsible for keeping the peace, many eldest daughters grow into people-pleasers. it wasn’t a choice; it was survival. if everyone else was happy, maybe you could finally exhale. if everything was calm, maybe you could rest. peace became something you had to earn through performance. 

## **reason 2: the lost childhood** 

when you spend your childhood parenting, you lose something you can never get back: the right to be carefree, messy, soft, unprepared. the right to not know. you traded dolls for diapers, play for responsibility, innocence for vigilance. and that grief is real. 

maybe no one ever let you grieve it. maybe you were told, _“that’s just how it is,”_ or, _“you should be grateful you turned out strong.”_ but strength doesn’t cancel loss. being parentified means your childhood ended too early. part of 

12 

this journey is giving yourself permission to name that loss, to say: i should have had more time to just be a kid. 

this grief isn’t about blaming your family, it’s about telling the truth: that you were put in roles that were never yours to carry. that you were trained to believe love is earned through sacrifice. that you forgot your own needs in order to meet everyone else’s. 

## **reason 3: you were the blueprint, aka. the trial kid** 

as the firstborn, you are the blueprint. parents try their hardest (and make their biggest mistakes) with you. younger siblings watch you and follow the path you carve. firstborns are the ones who made the parents, parents. 

but for many eldest daughters, being the “trial child” overlapped with being the **“struggle baby.”** you weren’t just the one they experimented on, you were the one who lived through your parents’ hardest years. poverty, migration, abuse, or instability weren’t abstract stories told later; they were your reality. you watched your parents fight to survive, and some of that survival became your responsibility too. 

this messes with your sense of self: every achievement feels like a duty, every failure like a warning. you’re not just living your life, you’re carrying the unspoken command to “do well so your siblings can do better.” 

13 

## **reason 4: gendered expectations** 

the burden isn’t just birth order, it’s gendered. daughters are more often expected to do the unseen work: caring for siblings, translating for immigrant parents, keeping the household running. while brothers might get a pass, daughters inherit the responsibility to hold the family together. 

you may have been called _“bossy,” “naggy,”_ or _“second mom.”_ but what you really were? overworked. 

## **reason 5: unrealistic expectations and pressure to succeed** 

being the eldest daughter often means carrying the weight of expectations no one ever said out loud but everyone enforced. you were the report card your parents showed the world. you were the proof of their sacrifices. every grade, every trophy, every achievement wasn’t just about you, it was about them. 

so the pressure to succeed was relentless. excellence wasn’t celebrated; it was required. mistakes weren’t lessons; they were failures. and when you’re raised under that kind of microscope, you learn to tie your worth to performance. if you achieve, you are lovable. if you fall short, you are nothing. 

this is why so many eldest daughters grow into perfectionists and overachievers. the praise feels fleeting, the criticism cuts deep, and _“good enough”_ never feels good enough. you learn to sprint on a treadmill that never stops, chasing approval that always moves one step further away. 

and yet, when you pause long enough, the question rises: who am i when i’m not performing, not achieving, not managing?

## **reason 6: emotional wounds** 

## **(mother, father, grandmother)** 

the eldest daughter wound doesn’t just come from what you had to do, it also comes from what you didn’t receive. love, safety, guidance. the places where your parents’ own wounds spilled over onto you. 

the mother wound can look like never feeling truly seen by her, being her mirror instead of her child, or carrying the weight of her unhealed pain. maybe she leaned on you as her confidant, or maybe she pushed you harder because no one ever gave her softness. either way, you learned love as pressure, not rest. 

the father wound often shows up as absence, whether physical or emotional. maybe he couldn’t protect you, maybe you had to protect him. some eldest daughters end up _“mothering”_ their own fathers, carrying burdens no child should carry. and because of that, receiving love or support from men later in life can feel foreign, almost dangerous. 

the grandmother wound can come if she stepped into a maternal role, leaving no space for you to simply be a grandchild. or maybe she carried her own unhealed trauma forward, layering another generation’s pain onto your shoulders. instead of nurture and comfort, you inherited silence, resilience, or expectations. 

these wounds teach eldest daughters that even love is work. that connection comes with a cost. and those lessons don’t just disappear when you grow up, they echo into how you parent, how you partner, and how you see yourself. 

15 

## **reason 7: dethronement and sibling rivalry** 

for many eldest daughters, the wound begins the moment a younger sibling arrives. one day, you’re the center of your parents’ world, their only child. the next, you’re “the big sister,” expected to share not just toys, but attention, affection, and time. 

what no one tells you is how destabilizing that can feel. psychologists call it dethronement, the sudden loss of your position as the only child. to a young brain, it can feel like being replaced. anxiety creeps in: if they love the baby more, will they stop loving me? 

some eldest daughters respond by overachieving, trying to earn back love with gold stars and good behavior. others bury the jealousy deep, smiling on the outside while quietly resenting the sibling who “stole” their parents. many do both. 

that fear of being replaced doesn’t stay in childhood. it can follow you into adulthood: jealousy in friendships, anxiety in relationships, the constant worry that someone better, easier, or “less work” will take your place. 

the dethronement wound convinces you love is fragile, conditional, and always one mistake away from being lost. and so you grip harder, perform better, give more, hoping it will be enough to keep you chosen. 

the trap of the eldest daughter wound 

it convinces you that carrying more makes you strong and independent. but what it really makes you is exhausted. 

16 

psychologist gabor maté puts it this way: 

_“when you have been forced to suppress your needs long enough, you forget they exist.”_ 

you forget you were ever allowed to have needs at all. you forget that your worth isn’t in the meals you cooked, the grades you earned, the siblings you raised, or the chaos you calmed. you forget there was once a little girl inside you who deserved safety, softness, and love without conditions. 

those needs are still there. that girl is still there. and the work ahead isn’t about blaming the past, it’s about remembering yourself again.

## **why pretending you’re fine isn’t working** 

like every other human in this world, by the time you enter adulthood, you have a host of beliefs that your inner child tightly holds on to. some are positive, some are negative. 

for people who had emotionally healthy and present parents, their inner child has a mostly positive set of beliefs. armed with a positive self-belief and a realistic view of life, they can weave through life’s challenges with the emotional confidence, resilience and adaptability needed to thrive. that’s what sophia came to learn from her therapist. 

on the other hand, for eldest daughters who grew up with neglect, unrealistic expectations, or parentification, their inner beliefs are shaped by duty and fear. some learn, “i am only loved when i’m useful.” others cling to perfectionism, convinced mistakes erase their worth. some even create an unrealistically positive mask to hide the pain, a way to convince themselves and others that everything is fine. either way, these patterns form a distorted view of themselves and the world, leaving them struggling to deal with challenges in healthy ways. 

research backs this up. a study published in the international journal of qualitative studies in health and well-being (sjöblom et al., 2016) found that our inner child, or in this case, the eldest daughter self, remains present throughout our lives. it influences how we face difficulties, how we interpret pain, and even whether we can turn something bad into something good. 

19 

during challenging moments, an unhealed eldest daughter self often makes the situation worse. 

think of her not just as a younger version of you, but as a tool. a well-caredfor tool works smoothly. a broken one causes damage. a healed eldest daughter self can empower you to make sound decisions, respond rationally, and build healthy connections. she gives you the confidence to rest without guilt, to set boundaries, to trust others. with her healed, you can enjoy meaningful relationships, success that doesn’t cost your well-being, and a life that feels like yours. 

but when that eldest daughter self is unhealed, fragmented, and still burdened, she can pull you back into old patterns. she drives you to overfunction, to distrust others, to make decisions out of fear. she fuels the peoplepleasing, the perfectionism, the self-sabotage. maybe you accuse a partner of leaving you, not because they showed signs, but because you still carry the fear of abandonment from childhood. maybe you overspend to cover shame about your family’s struggles, repeating patterns that keep you insecure. 

as long as your eldest daughter self clings to the past instead of being healed, she will keep running the show, and you’ll keep paying the price. she will keep sabotaging your health, your goals, your happiness, and your future. 

as christine langley-obaugh put it: 

_“we repeat what we don’t repair.”_

## **older sister guilt** 

you and your younger sister were at home alone one day after school. your parents would be home in an hour or two, and they entrusted you with ensuring that the house wasn’t burned down. that meant cleaning the place up, doing the dishes… and making sure your sister was okay. 

she was playing near a table, atop which sat one of your mother’s cherished vases. you saw her bump it once and immediately told her not to play there, for fear of the vase shattering. you got on with the dishes, humming along to a song in your head when - crash. 

you knew exactly what it was without even having to see it. 

coming back into the room, your eyes dart from your sister to the shattered remains of the vase, back to your sister. she looks totally… clueless about what she’s done - so you make sure that she knows, as loud and as angrily as possible. 

your face is flushed, your throat hurts a little from shouting, your hands have clenched into balls - you know that you’re perfectly justified in your anger. and yet… 

you simply can’t stand the sight of your baby sister crying. 

wiping away the tears of your own that have just begun to well up in your eyes, you go over to her, wrapping her up in your arms, suddenly completely ashamed of having been so angry. 

this is hardly the last time in your life that this happens.

that cycle of anger, then immediate shame, then the weight of responsibility again, is the eldest daughter’s constant companion. 

what you felt in that moment with your sister is what so many eldest daughters carry every day: guilt. think back. when was the first time you remember feeling guilty for being angry, tired, for not being perfect, for existing? what’s the earliest memory you have of guilt taking over? 

notice how much of your guilt is actually borrowed or survival-based. that’s proof the voice in your head was never fully yours. this exercise is about dragging guilt out of the shadows and onto the page.
