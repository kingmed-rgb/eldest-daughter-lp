chapter 2. where it all started.

growing up with a highly critical mother had not been easy for sophia. whenever she slipped up, her mother, mel, would berate her and pile on guilt. mel would shame her and tell her she wasn’t trying hard enough.

failure was not an option in sophia’s home, not if she wanted her mom’s love and approval. and emotions? those were treated as weakness. when sophia cried after losing a volleyball game or felt crushed for not winning that music competition, mel would scold her. if sophia had just “done better,” as mel insisted, she wouldn’t feel so bad. according to her mother, the only way to earn peace and pride was to always win, never fail, never make mistakes. by adulthood, sophia looked like she had it all together: driven, dependable, always the one others leaned on. but inside, a part of her still carried the weight of being “the responsible one” since childhood. this was the eldest daughter wound. it was like those cartoon storylines where a tiny character sits at the controls of a much bigger body. for sophia, that little controller was the part of her that had grown up too fast, the “eldest daughter self” who had learned to survive by being perfect, useful, and responsible.

in psychology, this mirrors what many call the inner child, the part of you that holds on to childhood memories, fears, and unmet needs. for eldest daughters, that inner child often looks more like a little parent: the girl who had to grow up before she was ready, who learned that her worth came from caregiving, overachieving, and keeping the family together.

as children, we’re vulnerable. we rely on parents not just to feed us, but to show us that our emotions are valid and that we are safe. imagine a child falling in the park. if the mother comforts her, the child learns, “i’m safe. i’m loved. the world can be good.” but if the mother yells, dismisses, or shames her, the child learns, “i’m unworthy. my feelings don’t matter. the world isn’t safe.”

for eldest daughters, this cycle is intensified. instead of being comforted, they’re often told to “set a good example,” to be strong, or to stop making things harder for their parents. the message seeps in: love is conditional. worth must be earned. and once those beliefs take root in a young, malleable brain, they don’t let go easily.

this is why so many eldest daughters carry invisible weight into adulthood: still hustling for worth, still believing mistakes make them unlovable, still taking care of everyone else while ignoring their own needs.

so, how did we get here?

reason 1: parentification it begins with the invisible long list of unpaid jobs you never chose. being the eldest daughter means you are the default adult. the one who wakes siblings up for school, ties their shoes, remembers the birthdays, calms the fights, packs the lunches, and sometimes even consoles a parent when the weight of life crushes them. you become the “third parent” before you even know what childhood is supposed to feel like. sometimes they call you “too mature for your age,” and you thought that was something to be praised for.

psychologists call this parentification, when a child steps into adult roles because no one else can. eldest daughters are its most common victims. and while it can make you reliable, empathetic, and strong, it also leaves scars: exhaustion, hyper-independence, and the aching sense that rest is laziness. many eldest daughters never truly felt like children. some remember cooking meals at nine years old; others recall standing between their parents during fights, shouting “stop fighting!” childhood became something to manage, not something to live.

it’s not just the chores. it’s also the emotional labor: making sure your siblings are okay, checking if your parents are in a good mood, anticipating conflict before it erupts and adjusting yourself to prevent it. you learn to read rooms like a weather report, hyper-vigilant, constantly scanning for storms.

because you were always responsible for keeping the peace, many eldest daughters grow into people-pleasers. it wasn’t a choice; it was survival. if everyone else was happy, maybe you could finally exhale. if everything was calm, maybe you could rest. peace became something you had to earn through performance.

reason 2: the lost childhood when you spend your childhood parenting, you lose something you can never get back: the right to be carefree, messy, soft, unprepared. the right to not know. you traded dolls for diapers, play for responsibility, innocence for vigilance. and that grief is real.

maybe no one ever let you grieve it. maybe you were told, “that’s just how it is,” or, “you should be grateful you turned out strong.” but strength doesn’t cancel loss. being parentified means your childhood ended too early. part of this journey is giving yourself permission to name that loss, to say: i should have had more time to just be a kid.

this grief isn’t about blaming your family, it’s about telling the truth: that you were put in roles that were never yours to carry. that you were trained to believe love is earned through sacrifice. that you forgot your own needs in order to meet everyone else’s.

reason 3: you were the blueprint, aka. the trial kid as the firstborn, you are the blueprint. parents try their hardest (and make their biggest mistakes) with you. younger siblings watch you and follow the path you carve. firstborns are the ones who made the parents, parents.

but for many eldest daughters, being the “trial child” overlapped with being the “struggle baby.” you weren’t just the one they experimented on, you were the one who lived through your parents’ hardest years. poverty, migration, abuse, or instability weren’t abstract stories told later; they were your reality. you watched your parents fight to survive, and some of that survival became your responsibility too.

this messes with your sense of self: every achievement feels like a duty, every failure like a warning. you’re not just living your life, you’re carrying the unspoken command to “do well so your siblings can do better.”

reason 4: gendered expectations the burden isn’t just birth order, it’s gendered. daughters are more often expected to do the unseen work: caring for siblings, translating for immigrant parents, keeping the household running. while brothers might get a pass, daughters inherit the responsibility to hold the family together.

you may have been called “bossy,” “naggy,” or “second mom.” but what you really were? overworked.

reason 5: unrealistic expectations and pressure to succeed being the eldest daughter often means carrying the weight of expectations no one ever said out loud but everyone enforced. you were the report card your parents showed the world. you were the proof of their sacrifices. every grade, every trophy, every achievement wasn’t just about you, it was about them. so the pressure to succeed was relentless. excellence wasn’t celebrated; it was required. mistakes weren’t lessons; they were failures. and when you’re raised under that kind of microscope, you learn to tie your worth to performance. if you achieve, you are lovable. if you fall short, you are nothing.

this is why so many eldest daughters grow into perfectionists and overachievers. the praise feels fleeting, the criticism cuts deep, and “good enough” never feels good enough. you learn to sprint on a treadmill that never stops, chasing approval that always moves one step further away.

and yet, when you pause long enough, the question rises: who am i when i’m not performing, not achieving, not managing?

reason 6: emotional wounds (mother, father, grandmother) the eldest daughter wound doesn’t just come from what you had to do, it also comes from what you didn’t receive. love, safety, guidance. the places where your parents’ own wounds spilled over onto you.

the mother wound can look like never feeling truly seen by her, being her mirror instead of her child, or carrying the weight of her unhealed pain. maybe she leaned on you as her confidant, or maybe she pushed you harder because no one ever gave her softness. either way, you learned love as pressure, not rest. the father wound often shows up as absence, whether physical or emotional. maybe he couldn’t protect you, maybe you had to protect him. some eldest daughters end up “mothering” their own fathers, carrying burdens no child should carry. and because of that, receiving love or support from men later in life can feel foreign, almost dangerous.

the grandmother wound can come if she stepped into a maternal role, leaving no space for you to simply be a grandchild. or maybe she carried her own unhealed trauma forward, layering another generation’s pain onto your shoulders. instead of nurture and comfort, you inherited silence, resilience, or expectations.

these wounds teach eldest daughters that even love is work. that connection comes with a cost. and those lessons don’t just disappear when you grow up, they echo into how you parent, how you partner, and how you see yourself.

reason 7: dethronement and sibling rivalry for many eldest daughters, the wound begins the moment a younger sibling arrives. one day, you’re the center of your parents’ world, their only child. the next, you’re “the big sister,” expected to share not just toys, but attention, affection, and time.

what no one tells you is how destabilizing that can feel. psychologists call it dethronement, the sudden loss of your position as the only child. to a young brain, it can feel like being replaced. anxiety creeps in: if they love the baby more, will they stop loving me?

some eldest daughters respond by overachieving, trying to earn back love with gold stars and good behavior. others bury the jealousy deep, smiling on the outside while quietly resenting the sibling who “stole” their parents. many do both.

that fear of being replaced doesn’t stay in childhood. it can follow you into adulthood: jealousy in friendships, anxiety in relationships, the constant worry that someone better, easier, or “less work” will take your place.

the dethronement wound convinces you love is fragile, conditional, and always one mistake away from being lost. and so you grip harder, perform better, give more, hoping it will be enough to keep you chosen.

the trap of the eldest daughter wound it convinces you that carrying more makes you strong and independent. but what it really makes you is exhausted.

psychologist gabor maté puts it this way:

“when you have been forced to suppress your needs long enough, you forget they exist.”

you forget you were ever allowed to have needs at all. you forget that your worth isn’t in the meals you cooked, the grades you earned, the siblings you raised, or the chaos you calmed. you forget there was once a little girl inside you who deserved safety, softness, and love without conditions.

those needs are still there. that girl is still there. and the work ahead isn’t about blaming the past, it’s about remembering yourself again.

chapter summary the eldest daughter wound didn’t come from nowhere. it was built through parentification, lost childhood, unrealistic expectations, gendered roles, emotional wounds, and dethronement. you didn’t imagine it. the weight you carry today was planted years ago. naming that truth is the first step toward setting it down.
