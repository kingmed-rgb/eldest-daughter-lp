The workbook

How many times have you been called "the baby"? At family dinners. At thirty. In front of people you were trying to impress.

It sounds sweet. Harmless, even. But for a lot of us, it came with an unspoken deal: stay small, stay fun, and let everyone else handle the serious things.

Your older siblings were trusted. You were protected. They got responsibility. You got rescued.

Being the fun one was a strategy

You learned early that making people laugh kept you safe. Being cute got you attention. Charm could get you out of trouble. Performance could fill a room so you wouldn't feel invisible.

And it worked. For a while.

But here's the trap: what saved you as a child is exhausting you as an adult.

Because one day you look up and you're the grown woman who still calls home before every big decision. Who freezes at bills and appointments. Whose ideas get a smile and a pat on the head instead of a real answer.

The signs you're still carrying it

Read these slowly. Be honest.

  • People still call you "the baby" well into adulthood.
  • Tasks other adults handle easily feel heavy: bills, appointments, confrontation.
  • You rely on charm to get out of difficult situations.
  • You look for approval before almost every decision.
  • Your family still treats you like you can't handle "adult" things.
  • You've been called "the fun one," but secretly feel empty.
  • You're afraid of being boring.
  • You don't know who you are without your family role.

If you checked even a few, this isn't a personality flaw. It's a role you were handed, and you never got to put it down.

I know because I lived it

I was always "the fun one." The one who could charm her way through anything, the one everyone called when they needed cheering up.

But somewhere around 28, something cracked. I couldn't make simple decisions without calling my mom three times. I kept turning to my oldest brother to bail me out of things I knew I could handle.

Reading about it helped me name it. It didn't help me change it. Changing it took writing things down, practicing small decisions on my own, and learning the difference between being supported and being rescued.

So I wrote the workbook I needed then.

What's inside

It isn't about fixing yourself overnight. It's about walking, step by step, into a lighter way of living, in three phases:

Phase oneAwarenessName the pattern: being treated as "the baby," the learned helplessness, the credibility gap that follows you everywhere.
Phase twoReleaseLet go of charm as survival, the need for outside approval, reactive rebellion, and the resentment you've been carrying.
Phase threeReclaimingStep into your competence. Set boundaries. Trust your own judgment.
